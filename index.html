Guideline Summary

Major international guidelines for managing hyperphosphatemia in chronic kidney disease — consolidated.

🌐 KDIGO 2024 Update

The 2024 KDIGO guidelines continue to suggest lowering elevated phosphate toward the normal range, but emphasize a more comprehensive "3D" approach — Diet, Drugs, and Dialysis — extending beyond traditional phosphate binder therapy. Treatment decisions should be based on serial measurements of phosphate, calcium, and PTH considered together, not isolated values.

🇺🇸 KDOQI

KDOQI recommends maintaining serum phosphorus at or above 2.7 mg/dL and no higher than 4.6 mg/dL in CKD stages 3–4. For dialysis patients (stage 5), the target is 3.5–5.5 mg/dL. The 2020 nutrition update suggests a lower normal-range goal of 2.5–4.5 mg/dL for hemodialysis patients.

🇬🇧 NICE (NG203)

NICE recommends calcium acetate as first-line phosphate binder for adults with stage 4–5 CKD and hyperphosphatemia, alongside dietary management. If calcium acetate is not tolerated or contraindicated, sevelamer carbonate should be offered. Calcium carbonate may be considered if calcium acetate is not tolerated.

🇯🇵 JSDT 2025

The Japanese Society for Dialysis Therapy released its 2025 revision of the CKD-MBD guideline in December 2025 — the first update in 13 years. It emphasizes individualized treatment, controlling phosphate and calcium first then PTH, and recommends dietary phosphate restriction to ≤700 mg/day with adequate dialysis and active vitamin D adjustment.

🔑 Central paradox across all guidelines: Despite universal recommendations to lower phosphate, there is a lack of definitive RCT evidence that phosphate-lowering improves hard clinical outcomes such as mortality or cardiovascular events. The 2025 Cochrane review judged the evidence certainty as low or very low for most comparisons. This uncertainty applies to the entire class of phosphate-lowering therapies, including binders and tenapanor.

KDIGO Guidelines

Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes — 2017 update with 2024 refinements.

Core Recommendations

4.1.1 — Assessment basis▶
In patients with CKD G3a–G5D, treatments of CKD-MBD should be based on serial assessments of phosphate, calcium, and PTH levels, considered together (Not graded).
4.1.2 — Phosphate lowering▶
In patients with CKD G3a–G5D, lower elevated phosphate levels toward the normal range (2C). This unified recommendation applies across all CKD stages — previously, G5D had a different threshold.
4.1.4 — Phosphate binders▶
In patients with CKD stages 3–5 (2D) and 5D (2B), use phosphate-binding agents in the treatment of hyperphosphatemia.
4.1.8 — Dietary phosphate▶
In patients with CKD G3a–G5D, limit dietary phosphate intake alone or in combination with other treatments (2D). Consider phosphate source (animal, vegetable, additives) in making dietary recommendations (Not graded).
Calcium-based binder restriction▶
KDIGO 2017 suggested restricting the dose of calcium-based phosphate binders in adults with CKD G3a–G5D who have hypercalcemia, vascular calcification, or adynamic bone disease. The 2024 update continues this caution.
🧠 The "3D" framework: KDIGO 2024 explicitly organizes treatment around Diet (phosphate restriction), Drugs (phosphate binders, tenapanor, calcimimetics), and Dialysis (optimizing phosphate clearance).

KDIGO and Tenapanor

KDIGO 2017 did not include tenapanor (FDA approval came in 2023). The 2024 update acknowledges novel non-binder therapies. In practice, tenapanor is positioned as add-on therapy for patients with inadequate response to, or intolerance of, phosphate binders — aligned with KDIGO's principle of intensifying therapy when targets are not met.

Dietary Phosphorus Restriction

KDOQI recommends restricting dietary phosphorus to 800–1,000 mg/day (adjusted for dietary protein needs) when serum phosphorus is elevated.

⚠️ Practical challenge: KDOQI acknowledges the lack of sufficient evidence supporting phosphate-lowering therapy in patients with normal serum phosphate. The work group believes sufficient equipoise exists to justify a rigorous RCT comparing normalization of phosphate vs. permissive mild-to-moderate hyperphosphatemia in dialysis patients.

KDOQI on Calcium-Based Binders

The 2017 KDOQI commentary on KDIGO noted that while the updated guideline suggests avoidance of calcium-based phosphate binders, critical evidence gaps remain. For many US patients — especially those with limited prescription coverage — calcium-based binders may be the only feasible option due to cost.

NICE Guidelines (UK)

National Institute for Health and Care Excellence — NG203, updated 2021.

Phosphate Binder Recommendations

Adults (CKD stage 4–5)

  • First-line: Offer calcium acetate to control serum phosphate.
  • Second-line: If calcium acetate is contraindicated (e.g., hypercalcemia, low PTH) or not tolerated, offer sevelamer carbonate.
  • Third-line: Consider sucroferric oxyhydroxide (for adults on dialysis if a calcium-based binder is not needed) or calcium carbonate (if a calcium-based binder is needed).
  • Lanthanum carbonate should only be considered if other phosphate binders cannot be used.

Children and Young People (CKD stage 4–5)

Offer a calcium-based phosphate binder to control serum phosphate levels. Titrate dosage to achieve best possible phosphate control while keeping serum calcium below the upper normal limit.

📌 NICE vs. KDIGO difference: NICE places calcium acetate as first-line, while KDIGO 2017/2024 suggests restricting calcium-based binders. This reflects different weighting of cost-effectiveness vs. vascular calcification risk.

JSDT Guidelines (Japan)

Japanese Society for Dialysis Therapy — CKD-MBD guideline, 2025 revision (first in 13 years).

Key Features of the 2025 Revision

  • Individualization emphasized: Treatment targets are tailored to the individual patient rather than rigid population-level thresholds.
  • Control sequence: Phosphate and calcium are controlled first, then PTH levels are managed.
  • Dietary restriction: Phosphate intake ≤700 mg/day, with emphasis on food quality — choosing low phosphorus-to-protein ratio foods, avoiding processed foods with inorganic phosphate additives, and favoring plant-based proteins (soy) with lower absorption rates.
  • Tenapanor in practice: Real-world Japanese practice starts tenapanor at 10 mg/day (lower than the US 30 mg BID), with a mean dose of ~17 mg at 52 weeks, achieving phosphate control while reducing binder burden.

🇯🇵 Unique Japanese Approach

The JSDT guideline includes a distinctive 9-segment P/Ca management diagram (9分割図) for adjusting medications based on combined phosphate and calcium fractions — a more granular approach than Western guidelines.

🌱 Japanese real-world evidence: A 2025 open-label RCT (Suzuki et al.) in 90 hemodialysis patients found that low-dose tenapanor (starting 10 mg/day) significantly improved stool consistency and resolved constipation by week 5, with laxative use falling from 58.2% to 35.6% (p<0.01). Phosphate control was comparable, and lanthanum carbonate prescriptions were largely replaced by low-dose tenapanor.

Other Society Guidelines

Additional international and regional recommendations.

🇪🇺 ERBP (European Renal Best Practice)

Suggests using phosphate-binding agents in CKD stages 3–5 (2D) and 5D (2B). Recommends avoiding calcium-based binders in patients with vascular or valvular calcification — lanthanum carbonate or sevelamer preferred as first choice.

🇨🇦 Canadian Society of Nephrology

For children with CKD G2–G5D, recommends basing phosphate-lowering treatment choice on serum calcium levels. Calcium-based binders recommended when calcium is below age-specific upper normal limit.

🇨🇳 Chinese Expert Consensus 2025

Published July 2025, provides practical guidance on timing of intervention, target serum phosphate levels, dietary restriction, phosphate-lowering agents, secondary hyperparathyroidism control, adequate dialysis, and patient education.

🌏 Indian Commentary (KDIGO)

Suggests recommended targets for CKD stage 3–4 of 3–4.6 mg/dL, and for stage 5 of 3.5–5.5 mg/dL — consistent with KDOQI.

Treatment Algorithm

Stepwise approach to hyperphosphatemia in CKD.

Step 1 — Confirm persistent hyperphosphatemia

  • Measure serum phosphate, calcium, PTH, 25-OH vitamin D, and alkaline phosphatase starting at CKD stage 3a.
  • Treat only if phosphate is progressively or persistently elevated — do not treat normal levels with binders.
  • Assess trends, not isolated values. Consider all three parameters together.

Step 2 — Dietary phosphate restriction

  • Restrict dietary phosphate to 0.8–1 g/day (typical Western guideline) or ≤700 mg/day (JSDT).
  • Focus on phosphate quality: avoid processed foods with inorganic phosphate additives (absorption ~100%); favor plant-based proteins (absorption ~40%).
  • Ensure adequate protein intake to prevent malnutrition.

Step 3 — Optimize dialysis

  • For dialysis patients, ensure adequate dialysis dose before escalating pharmacotherapy.
  • Consider intensified dialysis regimens for persistent hyperphosphatemia in G5D.

Step 4 — Phosphate binders

  • Calcium-based first-line (NICE): Calcium acetate preferred; titrate to keep calcium below upper normal limit.
  • Non-calcium binders preferred (KDIGO/ERBP) if: hypercalcemia, vascular calcification, adynamic bone disease, or suspected calcium overload.
  • Combine calcium-based + non-calcium binders if phosphate persists despite maximum tolerated dose.

Step 5 — Add tenapanor (if eligible)

  • Indicated as add-on therapy for adults on dialysis with inadequate response to, or intolerance of, phosphate binders.
  • 30 mg BID in the US; Japanese practice starts at 10 mg/day and titrates based on response and GI tolerability.
  • Particularly rational in patients with coexisting constipation — the laxative effect becomes therapeutic.
  • Do not take right before hemodialysis; take before the next meal following dialysis.

Step 6 — Reassess and individualize

  • Monitor serum phosphate, calcium, and PTH regularly. Frequency depends on CKD severity and treatment changes.
  • If PTH is progressively rising or >9× upper limit of normal despite phosphate control and vitamin D repletion, consider active vitamin D analog or calcimimetic.
  • Do not correct PTH to normal — risks adynamic bone disease.
⚠️ Pediatric consideration: Tenapanor is contraindicated in children <6 years and should be avoided up to 12 years due to serious dehydration risk (boxed warning). For pediatric patients, use calcium-based and/or non-calcium binders with age-appropriate targets.

Tenapanor (Xphozah®)

Minimally absorbed NHE3 inhibitor — dual-purpose therapy for hyperphosphatemia and constipation.

Mechanism of Action

Tenapanor is a minimally absorbed sodium-hydrogen exchanger 3 (NHE3) inhibitor that reduces paracellular intestinal phosphate absorption. Its on-target effect of increasing stool sodium and water content makes it uniquely dual-purpose in dialysis patients with both hyperphosphatemia and constipation. It is not a phosphate binder — it works by a complementary mechanism.

FDA-Approved Indication (Xphozah)

✔ Indicated to reduce serum phosphorus in adults with CKD on dialysis as add-on therapy for patients with inadequate response to, or intolerance of, phosphate binders. Dose: 30 mg orally twice daily before the morning and evening meals, titrated to serum phosphorus and GI tolerability.
✘ Not approved for constipation as a primary indication. The IBS-C formulation (Ibsrela) is dosed higher at 50 mg twice daily.

Evidence for Phosphate Lowering

AMPLIFY — Tenapanor + Phosphate Binders (Dual-Mechanism)

Pergola et al., JASN 2021. Randomized trial of tenapanor + phosphate binders vs. binders alone in maintenance dialysis patients. Tenapanor added to binders produced greater phosphate reduction than binders alone, confirming additive benefit of the dual-mechanism approach.

Block et al. 2017 — Monotherapy in Hemodialysis

JASN 2017. Tenapanor monotherapy significantly lowered serum phosphate in hemodialysis patients compared with placebo. Established proof-of-concept for NHE3 inhibition as a phosphate-lowering strategy.

Suzuki et al. 2025 — Open-Label RCT (Japan)

90 hemodialysis patients randomized to tenapanor (started 10 mg/day, titrated) vs. standard care over 23 weeks.

  • Constipation: BSFS 1–2 resolved by week 5; laxative use fell from 58.2% to 35.6% (p<0.01)[citation:18]
  • Phosphate: Comparable control between groups[citation:18]
  • Binder burden: Lanthanum carbonate prescriptions largely replaced by low-dose tenapanor[citation:18]
  • Safety: Transient early loose stools; 10 patients discontinued for diarrhea[citation:18]

PHREEDOM — 52-Week Open-Label Study

Koiwa et al., Scientific Reports 2023. Long-term tenapanor maintained serum phosphorus within target while allowing substantial reduction/elimination of phosphate binders. No major safety problems identified; safety profile consistent with shorter-term studies.

Dietary Management

Phosphate restriction — the foundation of all guidelines.

🎯 Restriction Targets

  • MSD/KDIGO-based: 0.8–1 g/day for eGFR <60 mL/min/1.73m²
  • KDOQI: 800–1,000 mg/day (adjusted for protein needs)
  • JSDT: ≤700 mg/day

🧬 Phosphate Quality Matters

  • Inorganic phosphate additives (processed foods) — absorption ~100% — avoid
  • Animal protein — absorption ~60–70%
  • Plant protein (soy, legumes) — absorption ~40% — prefer

🚫 Foods to Limit

  • Dairy products (milk, cheese, yogurt, ice cream)
  • Processed meats with phosphate additives
  • Cola drinks (phosphoric acid)
  • Processed baked goods and convenience foods

✅ Practical Strategies

  • Cook at home with fresh ingredients
  • Read labels for "phos" additives (dicalcium phosphate, sodium hexametaphosphate)
  • Choose fresh fruits and vegetables (low-phosphate)
  • Work with a renal dietitian for individualized plan
⚠️ The protein-phosphorus dilemma: The majority of high-biological-value proteins have high phosphate content. It is not simple to devise a dietary plan that lowers phosphate without compromising protein intake. Maintaining adherence to low-phosphate diets is challenging.

What the Highest-Quality Evidence Shows

Phosphate binders lower serum phosphate — yes▶
All guidelines agree that phosphate binders effectively lower serum phosphate. The Cochrane 2025 review (134 studies, 20,913 adults) confirms this is the best-established effect.[citation:9]
Hard clinical outcomes — uncertain▶
The Cochrane review found low or very low certainty for all-cause mortality, cardiovascular death, and vascular calcification outcomes. Sevelamer may have little or no effect on death from any cause (RR 0.45, 95% CI 0.13–1.53; 6 studies, 781 participants; low-certainty evidence).[citation:9]
Tenapanor — phosphate lowering confirmed, outcomes not powered▶
Tenapanor significantly lowers serum phosphate as monotherapy and in combination with binders. However, no trial has been powered to show improvement in cardiovascular events or mortality as a primary endpoint.
Network meta-analysis rankings▶
In a 2025 network meta-analysis of 70 RCTs (15,551 dialysis patients), sucroferric oxyhydroxide and tenapanor ranked highest for lowering all-cause mortality based on SUCRA probabilities. Sevelamer was the only binder with a statistically significant mortality benefit vs. calcium-based agents (RR 0.59, 95% CI 0.37–0.94).[citation:4]
The critical evidence gap▶
The 2025 KDOQI commentary states: "There remains a lack of knowledge regarding the efficacy of phosphate binders on hard clinical outcomes, such as mortality... The KDOQI work group believes that sufficient equipoise exists to justify an adequately powered rigorously conducted clinical trial assessing the safety and efficacy of normalization of phosphate levels versus permissive mild to moderate hyperphosphatemia in hyperphosphatemic patients undergoing dialysis."

Cardiovascular Outcomes

Tenapanor vs. conventional phosphate binders — what the evidence does and doesn't show.

⚠️ No head-to-head trial has been powered to answer definitively whether tenapanor improves long-term cardiovascular outcomes compared with conventional phosphate binders. What exists are indirect comparisons, post-hoc analyses, and surrogate endpoints.

Cardiovascular Mortality: Indirect Signals

  • Network meta-analysis (2025, 70 RCTs): Sucroferric oxyhydroxide and tenapanor ranked highest for lowering all-cause mortality. Sevelamer was the only binder with a statistically significant mortality benefit vs. calcium-based binders (RR 0.59, 95% CI 0.37–0.94).[citation:4]
  • TEN-02-301 (26-week open-label): Deaths similar — 1.7% with tenapanor vs 2.2% with phosphate binders, mostly cardiovascular or sepsis.
  • PHREEDOM (52-week): Tenapanor associated with a smaller percentage of deaths and hospitalizations than sevelamer.

MACE and Hospitalizations

  • FDA review: Early concern about venous thromboembolism (4.7% vs 0% on placebo) and significant cardiovascular AEs (8% vs 0.7% on placebo) in an early trial.
  • TEN-02-301 (larger controlled trial): Serious adverse events reported more frequently with sevelamer (16–23%) than tenapanor (11–17%) across study periods.
  • Specific CV AEs: Small numerical imbalances (acute respiratory failure 3.1% vs 0.7%; congestive cardiac failure 1.2% vs 0%) — not statistically significant.
